A programmable alphabet for aromatic materials.

Grapheins is building a new class of sequence-defined aromatic materials. They are chains of fused-aromatic building blocks, joined one unit at a time by the same amide bond that assembles every protein in biology.

How it works Get in touch
The idea

Biology builds with sequence. Chemistry mostly builds at random.

Proteins owe their precision to one thing: a small alphabet of building blocks arranged in a defined sequence, joined head-to-tail by amide bonds, folding into exact shapes. Most synthetic polymers, by contrast, are assembled randomly or by simple repetition.

Grapheins closes that gap from an unusual direction. Our building block is not a biological amino acid but a fused-aromatic core. In the first generation it is a well-characterized polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon, used as a manageable, low-cost surrogate for a fragment of nanographene. Each core carries two directional handles, one amino and one carboxyl, so the blocks couple head-to-tail through ordinary amide bonds, exactly as peptides do.

The result is a sequence-defined chain whose backbone presents flat, π-rich aromatic faces instead of biological side chains. It behaves like a molecular Lego, in which the identity and the order of every unit is programmed.

01

Molecular biomimicry

We treat a nanographene surrogate as if it were an amino acid, with defined N-to-C polarity, so decades of peptide-coupling chemistry become directly usable.

02

Sequence definition

Chains are built one unit at a time, in a programmed order. The sequence becomes the design variable: a vast, addressable space of π-conjugated materials.

03

Computation-to-lab selection

An in-silico screen ranks candidate sequences, so we synthesize only the few worth making and spend simulation cycles instead of reagents.

The opportunity

A large materials space that precision has barely reached.

π-conjugated aromatic materials sit behind much of modern technology, from displays and solar cells to sensors and advanced coatings. What has been hard is building them with the atom-by-atom control that biology takes for granted. Composition can be tuned, but the exact order of the units usually cannot.

By bringing sequence control to aromatic backbones, Grapheins aims to make that order a property a designer can set on purpose. These are the directions we find most interesting:

Organic and molecular electronics

Charge-transport behaviour in π-conjugated systems depends on how the aromatic units are arranged, not only on which units are present.

Optoelectronic materials

Light absorption and emission in aromatic chains are shaped by sequence and geometry, which sequence control is meant to address directly.

Molecular sensing and recognition

Defined sequences can, in principle, present specific surfaces and binding patterns, in the same spirit as a folded peptide.

To be explicit, and we prefer to say it plainly: these are directions, not results. We have not demonstrated any device, nor any functional property. The near-term work is deliberately narrow, and any real application is a long programme of validation away. We would rather state this clearly than imply more than we have shown.

Where we are, stated plainly

A complete design and preliminary simulations. Function still to be proven.

We hold ourselves to the same filter we apply to any claim: a molecule is worth nothing until it is synthesized, isolated, and its structure characterized beyond doubt. As of today, Grapheins has a complete molecular design and preliminary computational support. We do not claim any demonstrated functional property. Our next step is a focused proof of concept: to synthesize, purify and characterize a single main-chain amide bond between two aromatic building blocks, confirmed independently.

A lean research company, by design.
